# First aid

## Before anything else
Call your local emergency number first for anything serious: 911 in the US and Canada, 112 across Europe, 999 in the UK. Serious means heavy bleeding, trouble breathing, chest pain, a person who is unresponsive, a suspected stroke, a bad burn, a fall from height, or any doubt. Put the phone on speaker and follow the dispatcher. Check the scene is safe for you before you go to the person. Wear gloves if you have them.

## Severe bleeding
A deep cut or wound that will not stop bleeding. Press hard and directly on the wound with a clean cloth or your hand. Do not lift the cloth to look; add more on top if it soaks through. Keep pressing for at least ten minutes. Raise the limb if that does not hurt. If an object is embedded, press around it and do not pull it out. If a limb is bleeding so heavily that pressure fails and life is at risk, a tourniquet placed five to seven centimetres above the wound, tightened until the bleeding stops, can save a life; note the time and never loosen it. Watch for pale, cold, faint signs of shock.

## Minor cuts and grazes
Rinse under clean running water. Pat dry, apply gentle pressure until oozing stops, cover with a clean dressing. Change the dressing if it gets wet or dirty. See a professional if the cut is deep, gaping, on the face or a joint, from a bite, or if redness and swelling spread over the next days.

## Unresponsive and not breathing normally: CPR
Shout for help and call emergency services on speaker. Ask someone to find an AED. Kneel beside the person, heel of one hand on the centre of the chest, other hand on top, arms straight. Push hard and fast: about five centimetres deep, one hundred to one hundred and twenty pushes a minute, letting the chest come back up fully. If you are trained and willing, give two breaths after every thirty pushes; if not, keep pushing without stopping. When the AED arrives, turn it on and follow its voice. Do not stop until the person breathes, help takes over, or you cannot continue.

## Unresponsive but breathing: recovery position
Kneel beside them. Put the arm nearest you at a right angle, palm up. Bring the far arm across the chest and hold the back of that hand against the near cheek. Bend the far knee up. Pull on the knee to roll them toward you onto their side. Tilt the head back to keep the airway open. Check breathing every minute until help arrives. Do not leave them alone.

## Choking
If they can cough or speak, encourage coughing and stay with them. If they cannot breathe, cough or speak: give up to five sharp back blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand, then up to five abdominal thrusts, standing behind them with a fist above the navel pulled sharply inward and upward. Alternate five and five. Call emergency services if it does not clear. If they become unresponsive, start CPR. Anyone who has had abdominal thrusts should be checked by a professional.

## Burns and scalds
Cool the burn under cool running water for twenty minutes, starting as soon as possible. Do not use ice, butter, toothpaste or creams. Remove jewellery and loose clothing near the burn unless stuck to the skin. Cover loosely with cling film or a clean, non fluffy cloth. Do not burst blisters. Call emergency services for burns larger than the person's hand, on the face, hands, feet, genitals or joints, deep or white or charred burns, chemical or electrical burns, and any burn on a child or older person.

## Broken bones and dislocations
Suspect a break if there is severe pain, swelling, deformity, or the person cannot use the limb. Do not try to straighten it. Support it in the position found with padding, a sling for an arm, or by strapping a leg gently to the other leg. Apply a wrapped cold pack for swelling. Do not give food or drink in case surgery is needed. Call emergency services for an open break, a suspected broken hip, thigh, pelvis, back or neck, or a limb that is pale, cold or numb below the injury. Do not move someone with a possible spine injury unless they are in danger.

## Sprains and strains
Rest the joint. Ice or a cold pack wrapped in cloth for twenty minutes at a time, several times a day. Compress with an elastic bandage, snug but not tight. Elevate above the heart when resting. Have it checked if the person cannot put weight on it, the joint looks wrong, or it is no better in a couple of days.

## Head injury
Sit the person down and hold a cold pack to the injury. Watch them closely. Call emergency services for loss of consciousness, vomiting, confusion, worsening headache, unequal pupils, fluid from the ears or nose, seizure, weakness, slurred speech, or drowsiness that is hard to rouse. Anyone who was knocked out should be seen by a professional even if they feel fine. No alcohol, no driving, and someone should stay with them for the next day.

## Stroke: FAST
Face: ask them to smile, look for drooping on one side. Arms: ask them to raise both, look for one drifting down. Speech: ask them to repeat a simple sentence, listen for slurring or confusion. Time: if any of these, call emergency services at once and note the time symptoms started. Keep them still and comfortable, nothing to eat or drink. Every minute counts.

## Heart attack
Signs: pressure, tightness or pain in the chest that may spread to the arms, neck, jaw or back, with sweating, nausea, shortness of breath or light headedness. Women and older people may have milder signs. Call emergency services. Sit them down, knees bent, back supported. If they are not allergic and can swallow, one adult aspirin chewed slowly may help. Be ready to start CPR if they become unresponsive.

## Severe allergic reaction: anaphylaxis
Signs: swelling of the face, lips or tongue, difficulty breathing, wheeze, hives, faintness, especially after food, a sting or a medicine. Call emergency services. If they carry an adrenaline auto injector, use it now in the outer thigh, through clothing if needed, and hold for the time on the device. Lie them down with legs raised, or sit them up if breathing is hard. A second dose can be given after five minutes if there is no improvement. Always go to hospital afterwards, even if they recover.

## Seizure
Do not restrain them or put anything in the mouth. Move hard objects away, cushion the head, note the time. When the jerking stops, roll them into the recovery position and stay with them while they recover. Call emergency services if it lasts more than five minutes, repeats, they are injured, in water, pregnant, diabetic, it is their first seizure, or they do not come round.

## Shock
Shock is the body failing to circulate enough blood after severe bleeding, burns, allergy or injury. Signs: pale, cold, clammy skin, fast weak pulse, fast breathing, thirst, confusion, then drowsiness. Call emergency services. Treat the cause if you can. Lie them down and raise the legs unless there is a leg, head or spine injury. Keep them warm with a blanket or coat. Nothing to eat or drink. Reassure and keep checking breathing.

## Fainting
Lay them flat and raise the legs. Loosen tight clothing and give fresh air. They should come round within a minute or two. If they do not, treat as unresponsive. Sit them up slowly afterwards. Have them checked if fainting happens during exertion, with chest pain, or repeatedly.

## Hypothermia
Signs: shivering, then confusion, slurred speech, clumsiness, drowsiness, and shivering that stops. Get them out of wind and wet. Remove wet clothes and wrap in dry layers, a blanket or a sleeping bag, covering the head. Warm gradually, body to body if needed. Warm sweet drinks if they are fully awake. No alcohol, no rubbing of limbs, no direct heat on the skin. Handle gently. Call emergency services; severe hypothermia can look like death and still be survivable.

## Heat exhaustion and heat stroke
Heat exhaustion: heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, nausea, headache, cramps. Move to shade, lie them down, loosen clothing, cool with water on the skin and fanning, sip water or a sports drink. They should recover within thirty minutes. Heat stroke: hot skin, confusion, seizure, collapse, sweating may have stopped. That is an emergency: call for help and cool them fast with any water available, wet cloths on neck, armpits and groin, and fanning, until help arrives.

## Nosebleed
Sit them forward, not back. Pinch the soft part of the nose firmly for ten minutes without letting go to check. Breathe through the mouth. A cold pack on the bridge of the nose helps. Repeat once if needed. Seek help if it lasts more than thirty minutes, follows a blow to the head, or the person takes blood thinners.

## Bites and stings
Insect stings: scrape the sting out sideways with a card, wash, cold pack, watch for allergic reaction. Ticks: grip close to the skin with tweezers and pull straight out without twisting, clean the site, note the date. Animal or human bites: wash thoroughly under running water for several minutes, cover, and see a professional the same day; bites can infect and may need tetanus or rabies care. Snake bites: keep the person still and calm, keep the bitten limb below heart level and immobilised, remove rings and watches, call emergency services. Do not cut, suck, apply ice or a tourniquet, and do not try to catch the snake; a photo from a distance is enough.

## Eye injury
For dust or grit, blink and rinse with clean water; do not rub. For chemicals, rinse with running water for twenty minutes and get medical help. For a blow to the eye, hold a cold pack gently, no pressure. For anything embedded, do not remove it; cover both eyes loosely to limit movement and call emergency services.

## Poisoning and overdose
Call emergency services or the poison control line. Find out what was taken, how much and when; keep the container. Do not make them vomit. If they are drowsy or unresponsive, put them in the recovery position and watch their breathing. For something on the skin, rinse with water. For fumes, get them into fresh air. If an opioid overdose is possible and naloxone is available, use it and call for help.

## Diabetic emergency
Low blood sugar: shaky, sweaty, confused, irritable, drowsy. If they can swallow, give sugar: juice, sweets, glucose tablets, then a snack. If they do not improve in ten minutes or become unresponsive, call emergency services and use the recovery position. Never give food or drink to someone who cannot swallow safely.

## Asthma attack
Sit them upright and keep them calm. Help them take their reliever inhaler, one puff at a time, up to ten puffs, through a spacer if they have one. If no better after ten puffs, or they are getting worse, cannot speak in sentences, or lips look blue, call emergency services and repeat the puffs while you wait.
