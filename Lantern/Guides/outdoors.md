# Outdoors

## Lost: stop
Stop moving. Sit down, drink some water, and think. Panic and walking further are what turn a delay into a rescue. Observe: where did you last know exactly where you were, what can you see and hear, what does the weather look like, how long until dark. Plan: if you can retrace your steps with confidence, do so slowly. If not, stay where you are, especially if someone knows your route and you are due back. A person who stays put is found; a person who wanders is not.

## Calling for help
Try the emergency number even with no bars; another network may carry it. Text if a call fails. iPhone 14 and later can text emergency services by satellite: dial the emergency number, and when the call fails tap Emergency Text via Satellite and point the phone at the sky as it directs. Send your location to a contact by satellite from Find My if you can. Then put the phone on low power mode, airplane mode between attempts, and keep it warm against your body; a cold battery dies fast.

## Signalling
Three of anything means help: three whistle blasts, three shouts, three fires, three flashes. Repeat every few minutes. A whistle carries much further than a voice and costs no energy. Lay out bright clothing or gear in an open space in a large X or SOS. Use a mirror, a phone screen or any shiny surface to flash sunlight toward aircraft or distant people. At night, a torch pointed at the sky or a small fire in a clearing. Make yourself big and bright, not hidden under trees.

## Shelter and warmth first
Cold kills faster than hunger or thirst. Get out of wind and wet before dark. Use what is there: the lee side of a rock, a fallen tree, a dense thicket, a snow bank you can dig into. Put insulation between you and the ground: pack, foam, branches, leaves, a thick layer. Put on every dry layer, cover your head and neck, and share warmth with others. A survival blanket or bin bag over everything keeps wind and rain out. Keep boots on unless they are soaked. Light a fire only where it is safe and you can control it; small and fed often beats large.

## Hypothermia
Early signs: shivering, cold hands, clumsiness, irritability. Later: confusion, slurred speech, stumbling, shivering stops, wanting to sleep. Act early. Get out of wind and wet, change wet layers for dry, insulate from the ground, cover the head, eat something sugary, drink something warm if there is a way to heat it. Move gently. Do not rub the skin or give alcohol. Someone who is confused or has stopped shivering needs rescue; keep them warm and still and signal for help.

## Water
You can go days without food and only a few without water, less in heat. Ration sweat, not water: rest in shade in the heat of the day and move in the cool. Signs of dehydration: dark urine, headache, dizziness, tiredness. Sources: running streams, springs, rain caught in anything, dew wiped from grass at dawn, snow melted in a container, never eaten cold in quantity. Untreated water can carry parasites; if you have a way to boil for one minute, filter, or treat with tablets, do it. If not and you are days from help, drinking unsafe water beats collapse from dehydration; choose fast moving clear water away from animals.

## Heat and sun
Move early and late, rest in shade in the middle of the day. Cover skin with light loose clothing and a hat rather than stripping down. Sip water regularly. Heat exhaustion is heavy sweating, dizziness, nausea, cramps: stop, shade, cool the skin with water, drink. Heat stroke is hot skin, confusion, collapse: that is an emergency, cool them with anything you have and signal for help.

## Lightning
When thunder is within thirty seconds of a flash, the storm is close. Get off ridges, peaks and open water and away from lone trees and metal. Do not shelter under an overhang or in a shallow cave mouth. Spread the group out. Crouch on your pack or on the balls of your feet with heels together, head down, until thirty minutes after the last thunder. If someone is struck, they are safe to touch; start CPR if they are not breathing.

## Rivers and water crossings
Avoid if you can. Water above the knee and moving fast can take an adult off their feet. Look upstream and down for a wider, shallower, slower spot. Unclip pack straps so you can shed it. Face upstream, side step, use a pole or stick for a third point of contact. Cross as a group linked arm in arm. If swept away, feet up and downstream, on your back, and steer to the bank. A night in the open is safer than a bad crossing.

## Night
Do not move in the dark on unknown ground; falls are the most common serious injury. Make shelter before dusk. Keep the torch for signalling and essentials. Sleep in shifts if there is any danger. Start again at first light after eating and drinking something.

## Injuries on the trail
Sprain: rest, cold water or snow in a cloth, wrap firmly, raise. Walking out on a sprain with the boot laced tight is often possible; a suspected break is not. Blisters: cover with tape or a plaster before they burst, dry socks. Cuts: clean with water, cover, pressure for bleeding. Broken limb: pad and splint in the position found with sticks, a pole or a rolled mat, and signal for help rather than move far. Head, back or neck injury: do not move them unless in immediate danger.

## Animals
Most animals want to avoid you. Make noise as you walk in bear country and keep food sealed and away from where you sleep. If you meet a bear, do not run; stand tall, talk calmly, back away slowly. Snakes: watch where you put hands and feet, step onto logs not over them. If bitten, keep still and calm, immobilise the limb, get help; no cutting, sucking or ice. Ticks: check skin at the end of each day and remove with a straight pull.

## Finding direction
The sun rises roughly east and sets roughly west. Push a stick upright into flat ground and mark the shadow tip; wait fifteen minutes and mark again; the line between the marks runs west to east, first mark west. Water flows downhill and valleys usually lead to roads and people, but follow a stream only if the ground stays passable. A phone compass works without signal; the Maps app keeps its last loaded tiles and your blue dot works from GPS alone.

## Before you go next time
Tell someone your route and when you will be back. Carry a whistle, a torch, a survival blanket, a map, spare layers, more water than you think and something to eat, in a small bag you always take. Download offline maps for the area. Check the weather and turn back early rather than late.
